# VOICE.md

The voice guide for DamianMason.com. Every phase that writes copy writes against this file.

Everything here is derived from a measured corpus of Damian's own words on the live site. The corpus is **37 paragraphs, 126 sentences, 2,098 words**, pulled from:

- `_source/extracted/the-business-of-agriculture.txt` (latest-episode description, show description, meta description)
- `_source/extracted/do-business-better-podcast.txt` (three episode descriptions, the "Start Listening Today!" first-person paragraph)
- `_source/extracted/keynote.txt` (bio block, KEYNOTE/EXTENSIONS blurbs, FAQ answers)
- `_source/extracted/home.txt` (hero, "exceptional understanding" paragraph, "Connecting people" paragraph, full FAQ)
- `_source/extracted/boasg.txt` (group description, What you get / What you won't get)
- `_source/extracted/collaboration-opportunities.txt` (three role blurbs, "His favorite role?")
- `_source/extracted/meeting-coordinators.txt` (intro, "Repeat business" paragraph)
- `_source/extracted/product__food-fear-*.txt`, `product__do-business-better-*.txt` (book descriptions)
- `_source/extracted/xtreme-ag.txt`, `acres-tv.txt`, `reviews.txt`, `join-mailing-list.txt`, `contact-us.txt`

Excluded from the corpus: navigation, client testimonials (someone else's words), WooCommerce chrome, the Divi placeholder text, and the Lorem Ipsum FAQ items.

Every trait below is backed by a quote that is actually in those files. If a claim has no quote, it is not in this guide.

## 1. Who is speaking

Damian Mason is an agricultural economist who became a comedian, then put both to work on one job: telling farmers and agribusiness people the truth about their own industry, and making them laugh while he does it. Purdue Ag Econ degree. Second City Chicago, comedy writing and improv. Member of the Screen Actors Guild. He has been on stage since 1994: *"Since 1994, he has spoken to over 2,400 audiences in all 50 states and 7 foreign countries."* He owns a farm in Indiana. He quit a corporate job to do this on purpose, and he says so in the first person: *"I quit my Fortune 500 job in 1994 to pursue a life and business of my choice."* The credentials are not decoration. They are the reason he gets to be blunt. An economist can say the number, a farm owner can say what the number does to a family, and a comedian can say both without the room getting heavy.

The voice that comes out of that is an insider talking to insiders, at eye level, with no time to waste. He assumes his reader already knows what a closing wheel is, what red ink means, and why acres matter. He does not sell agriculture back to agriculture, and he refuses to repackage information the reader already has: *"You don't need anyone telling you the weather forecast or the commodity prices, technology does that for you! What you need is a connection with real-world agriculture people and topics that inform, educate, and help you grow."* He is opinionated on purpose. The site's own subhead for the podcast says *"Damian has strong opinions and he's not afraid to share them. You can agree or disagree."* And underneath the bluntness there is genuine reverence for the work: he calls it *"the world's most important industry"* twice on the site and credits himself with the ability *"to stir up feelings of dignity and pride for people working in the most important industry in the world."* Write him as a man who is impatient with fluff and unembarrassed about loving the business he is in.

## 2. Voice traits

### 1. Plainspoken and declarative
He answers the question and stops. The single shortest sentence in the corpus is one word long.

> **"Wherever the client's event is scheduled."**
> (`home.txt` / `keynote.txt`, FAQ answer to "WHERE DO YOU TRAVEL?")

> **"Absolutely. The only caveat is that Damian will not speak while people are eating or tables are being cleared."**
> (`keynote.txt`, FAQ)

> **"Damian's fees are NET to him."**
> (`home.txt`, FAQ)

**So do this:** Answer in the first sentence. Put the qualifier in the second sentence or leave it out. Never warm up before the point.

### 2. Contractions carry the sentence
The corpus splits cleanly in two. Damian's own podcast and first-person prose runs **1.62 contractions per 100 words**. The agency-written marketing and card copy on the same site runs **0.44**, nearly 4x lower. The flat parts of the current site are flat partly because nobody contracts anything.

> **"But this product design doesn't belong to his employer, it's his."**
> (`do-business-better-podcast.txt`, episode 142)

> **"When he's not traveling for work, Damian can be found on his Indiana farm with his wife Lori or escaping from winter at their Arizona residence."**
> (`keynote.txt`, bio)

> **"That's what doing business better is about."**
> (`do-business-better-podcast.txt`)

**So do this:** Target **1.5 to 2 contractions per 100 words**. If a paragraph of 60 words has zero contractions, you wrote it in the wrong register. Fix it before shipping.

### 3. Hard numbers do the persuading
He almost never reaches for an intensifier when a figure is available. The number is usually the first thing in the sentence, and the interpretation comes second.

> **"$2.6 billion. That's what U.S. cotton growers stand to lose this year according to USDA, the fifth straight year of red ink for the industry."**
> (`the-business-of-agriculture.txt`, latest episode)

> **"American consumers discard more than 70 pounds of apparel each year, nearly three fourths of which is made from petroleum based material."**
> (`the-business-of-agriculture.txt`)

> **"With more than 40,000 listeners per month, he will raise awareness of your brand's message as a featured podcast guest or sponsor."**
> (`collaboration-opportunities.txt`)

**So do this:** Before you write an adjective, check the source manifest for a number that says the same thing harder. Available and load-bearing: 2,400+ audiences, 50 states, 7 foreign countries, since 1994, 70,000+ views and downloads per month, 40,000+ listeners per month, $99/month, 60 to 90 minute programs, three decades of business ownership. Use the real figure or use nothing.

### 4. Dry humor that lives in the juxtaposition, not in a punchline
Nothing in the corpus is set up as a joke. The comedy is structural: two true things placed next to each other so the reader does the last step.

> **"'Eggflation' Gives Producers Record Profits While Internet Mocks Outrageous Prices"**
> (`hello-world.txt` / `blog.txt`, post title)

> **"Soybeans are having an incredible year. That's bad for prices."**
> (`blog-news.txt`, Straight Arrow News item)

> **"Dave Swain worked in corporate Agriculture his entire career until he jumped ship and started his own business (in his mid 50's no less!)."**
> (`do-business-better-podcast.txt`, episode 144)

> **"he has turned these topics into an interesting (and sometimes controversial) podcast"**
> (`the-business-of-agriculture.txt`)

**So do this:** Build the joke out of the facts, never out of a wink. State fact A, state fact B, stop. Do not add "who knew?", do not add an emoji, do not explain why it is funny. If you delete the humor and the sentence still carries information, you did it right.

### 5. Insider ag fluency, used without condescension
He never glosses a term, and he never brags about knowing it either. The reader is assumed competent.

> **"Why did Johnny Verell Take the 2×2 off his planter?"**
> (`xtreme-ag.txt`, linked article title. No explanation of what a 2x2 is, anywhere on the site.)

> **"In short, we've lost acres, we've lost infrastructure, and the producers have lost money."**
> (`the-business-of-agriculture.txt`)

> **"an information-packed community of ag professionals that span the industry, from pig farmers to beet growers, to ag finance and planning"**
> (`boasg.txt`)

**So do this:** Use the trade word and move on. No parenthetical definitions, no "as farmers know", no "in the world of agriculture". The one thing he does spell out is a proper noun the reader may not have met, and he spells it out in one clause: *"his company, Vision Technology Management, was born from his vision to..."*

### 6. Contrarian framing, with a payoff
He sets up against the expected position, then delivers something usable. The contrarian move is never just an attitude.

> **"You don't need any more Ag people discussing weather or commodity prices, you have that information on your phone!"**
> (`the-business-of-agriculture.txt`, meta description, quoted as his own words)

> **"While other business books claim to tell you how to reach success, they fall short because they don't address the fact that success is different for each of us."**
> (`product__do-business-better-...txt`)

> **"What you won't get: High-pressure sales tactics or constant pitches. Multiple emails each day clogging up your inbox. Unusable fluff."**
> (`boasg.txt`)

> **"We do have a few bureaus whom we have successfully worked with over the years, but we do not endorse them."**
> (`home.txt`, FAQ)

**So do this:** Where the page has an obvious default claim, name the default and refuse it, then say what you offer instead. One refusal per page maximum. He is contrarian, not sour.

### 7. Second person, aimed at a named job
"You" and "your" run at **2.05 per 100 words** across the corpus. He does not address "professionals" or "stakeholders." He addresses a list of actual occupations.

> **"This podcast is for you: the entrepreneur, business owner, solopreneur, self employed striver, or business person who wants to do it better. By better I mean more profitably, more happily, and for as many years as you choose."**
> (`do-business-better-podcast.txt`)

> **"Hire a Speaker that Works for YOU."**
> (`meeting-coordinators.txt`, eyebrow)

> **"If you're looking for a community to join where you get take-aways at every turn, this is it."**
> (`boasg.txt`)

> **"Face it, we all feel the pressure to innovate with new tech but sometimes we find implementation or full utilization so daunting, we revert back to what we know."**
> (`do-business-better-podcast.txt`, episode 144)

**So do this:** Every section of body copy should contain at least one "you" or "your." When you describe the audience, list the jobs: growers, pork producers, ag lenders, agronomists, meeting planners, association directors. "Face it," and "we all" are his tools for standing in the reader's shoes rather than above them.

### 8. Named specifics instead of categories
People, companies, states, brands, dates. The corpus contains 36 uses of a person's proper name.

> **"Plains Cotton Growers CEO, Kody Bessent joins cotton farmers Todd Kimbrell and Matt Miles."**
> (`the-business-of-agriculture.txt`)

> **"Matt Roeder is an Iowa farm kid who's employed as an Agricultural engineer for a plastics manufacturing company."**
> (`do-business-better-podcast.txt`, episode 142)

> **"Brad acquired the rights to the brand and in February 2023, will release the first batch of Alps Brau since 1978!"**
> (`do-business-better-podcast.txt`, episode 141)

> **"After speaking to companies such as Merck, Land O'Lakes, and Cargill"**
> (`product__do-business-better-...txt`)

**So do this:** Name the client, the state, the crop, the year. "Companies around the globe" is weaker than "Merck, Land O'Lakes, and Cargill" and you already have the second one.

### 9. Stakes and pride, stated without sentiment
He believes the industry matters and says so flatly. He never dresses it up in imagery.

> **"Damian speaks to companies around the globe that have a role in the world's most important industry: Agriculture."**
> (`keynote.txt`)

> **"Damian has an uncanny ability to stir up feelings of dignity and pride for people working in the most important industry in the world."**
> (`home.txt`)

> **"Motivating and inspiring audiences in both 'good' and 'bad' Ag climates."**
> (`keynote.txt`, eyebrow)

**So do this:** State the stake as a fact, in one clause, and then get back to business. No sunsets, no "heartland," no "feeding the world" unless there is a number attached to it.

## 3. Sentence mechanics

Measured on the 37-paragraph, 126-sentence, 2,098-word corpus described above.

| Measure | Value | What to do |
|---|---|---|
| Mean sentence length | **16.7 words** | Aim 15 to 18 on average |
| Median sentence length | **17 words** | |
| Range | **1 to 41 words** | Do not flatten to one length |
| Sentences of 8 words or fewer | **18 of 126 (14%)** | About one short punch per paragraph |
| Sentences of 15 words or fewer | **57 of 126 (45%)** | Nearly half should be short |
| Sentences over 35 words | **5 of 126 (4%)** | One long sentence per page, at most |
| Sentences per paragraph | mean **3.4**, median **3**, range **1 to 9** | Target 2 to 4 sentences |
| Words per paragraph | mean **57** | Cap web body paragraphs at ~70 words |

**Questions.** 7 of 126 sentences (**6%**) are questions. 4 of 37 paragraphs (**11%**) open with one. Real examples: *"Keynote not enough?"*, *"Are you an Ag professional who could benefit from outside perspectives, ideas, insights, and vision?"*, *"His favorite role?"*, *"Will the Great American Cotton Plan Save U.S. Cotton?"* Use a question to open a section, not to fill a paragraph. Roughly one per page.

**Fragments.** 6 of 126 sentences (**5%**) are fragments, and every one of them is doing work: *"$2.6 billion."*, *"You've found it."*, *"Keynote not enough?"*, *"No problem!"*, *"Absolutely."*, *"His favorite role?"* Fragments appear at the start of a paragraph as a hook or at the end as a landing. Never in the middle.

**Sentence-opening conjunctions and adverbs.** 11 of 126 (**9%**) open with a connective: *"In short,"*, *"Face it,"*, *"But this product design..."*, *"That's when he discovered..."*, *"Also, he puts in a bit of feel good facts..."*, *"And, he provides business reality and opportunity coverage..."*, *"Yes, Damian will work with you..."*, *"Absolutely."* Starting a sentence with And or But is in-voice. Do it about once per section.

**Punctuation habits.**

- **Semicolons: zero in 2,098 words.** He has never used one on this site. Do not introduce one.
- **Colons: 11 across 37 paragraphs**, roughly one every three paragraphs. The colon is his signature move, used to introduce a definition, a list, or a reveal: *"the two subjects he knows best: Business and Agriculture"*, *"the world's most important industry: Agriculture"*, *"This podcast is for you: the entrepreneur, business owner, solopreneur..."*, *"The result: a food book that is factual, informative, and entertaining."*, *"First step: contact Damian to make sure he has your event date available."*
- **Exclamation points: 9 in 2,098 words**, about one every 233 words. 6 sentences (5%) end in one. They cluster on offers and enthusiasm, never on data. Cap: one per section, zero in any paragraph that contains a statistic.
- **Parentheses: 4 in the whole corpus**, and all four are asides with a wink or a caveat, never citations: *"(in his mid 50's no less!)"*, *"(he still farms on the side)"*, *"(and sometimes controversial)"*, *"(Let's talk about bulk pricing!)"*
- **Comma splices: he uses them and they read as speech.** *"You don't need anyone telling you the weather forecast or the commodity prices, technology does that for you!"* and *"Damian Mason has an exceptional understanding of the Agriculture industry, he's been involved in it his entire life."* Keep them occasional and deliberate.
- **Capitalization.** He capitalizes *Agriculture*, *Ag*, *Agribusiness*, and sometimes *Industry* mid-sentence. This is a real and consistent habit, 15 instances of capitalized *Agriculture* in the corpus. Follow it.
- **Serial comma.** The source is inconsistent (*"food, fuel, and fiber"* but *"entertaining, informative and memorable"*). Standardize on the serial comma across the rebuild.

## 5. Banned list

### Em dashes: never, anywhere
No `U+2014`, no `&mdash;`, no `--`, and no ` - ` doing the job of a dash. The current site is littered with them and the fix is always one of three characters:

- Use a **period** when the clause can stand alone. `Keynote not enough? No problem!`
- Use a **comma** for a light aside. `Brad, a novice Indiana historian, was poking around the internet.`
- Use a **colon** when the second half defines, lists, or reveals the first. `The result: a food book that is factual, informative, and entertaining.`

The colon is the in-voice substitute and he already uses it constantly. Prefer it.

### Banned words and phrases

| Banned | Why | Write instead |
|---|---|---|
| "in today's fast-paced world" | Opens nothing, says nothing | Start with the fact |
| "delve" | Not a word he owns | look at, get into, dig into |
| "landscape" (figurative) | Ag has a literal landscape; do not waste the word | the market, the industry, the business |
| "unlock" | **Literally on the current site**: *"Unlock exciting collaboration opportunities"* (collaboration-opportunities meta description) | get, open, start |
| "elevate" | **Also on the current site**: *"Elevate your brand"* (same meta description) | grow, raise, put in front of |
| "seamless" | **Also on the current site**: *"From inquiry to the day of event the process is seamless."* (home, Flawless Process card) | Say what actually happens |
| "robust" | Consultant filler | strong, tested, or a number |
| "leverage" (as a verb) | Consultant filler | use, put to work |
| "game-changer" | Claim without evidence | Say what changed and by how much |
| "dive into" / "deep dive" | Not his register | get into, cover, discuss |
| "navigate the complexities" | Empty | Name the actual problem |
| "at the end of the day" | Filler | Delete |
| "it's not just X, it's Y" | Manufactured profundity | State Y. Stop. |
| "whether you're X or Y" | Hedged address; he names the job instead | *"This podcast is for you: the entrepreneur, business owner, solopreneur..."* |
| Tricolon padding ("thought-provoking, engaging, and inspiring") | Adjectives stacked to fill space | One adjective, or a number, or nothing |
| Any sentence that could open a LinkedIn post | Wrong register entirely | Rewrite as something he would say from a stage |

**The LinkedIn test, concretely.** Banned openers: "Let's talk about...", "Here's the thing.", "I'll be honest.", "Most people get this wrong.", "Three lessons from...", "The best advice I ever got...". Also banned: single-sentence paragraphs stacked for rhythm, and any sentence that ends in a question the writer immediately answers.

**One more.** Do not write "Damian is passionate about agriculture." The word "passionate" does not appear in the corpus once. He demonstrates it with 2,400 audiences and a farm.

## 7. Headline patterns

Six formulas, each derived from a heading that already works on the site.

**1. The conditional claim.**
Model: *"If it's Agriculture, it Needs Damian."* (home, xtreme-ag, acres-tv)
Formula: `If it's [category], it needs [subject].`
Short, cocky, provable. Works as a section header on any page where Damian is the answer.

**2. Statement, then reversal.**
Model: *"Soybeans are having an incredible year. That's bad for prices."* (blog-news)
Also: *"'Eggflation' Gives Producers Record Profits While Internet Mocks Outrageous Prices"* (blog)
Formula: `[Good news, plainly stated]. [Why that's a problem].`
This is his humor engine and his analysis engine at once. Best on podcast episodes, blog items, and any content card.

**3. The real question, asked plainly.**
Model: *"Will the Great American Cotton Plan Save U.S. Cotton?"* (podcast)
Also: *"Why did Johnny Verell Take the 2×2 off his planter?"* (xtreme-ag)
Formula: `[Will / Why / What] + [named thing] + [what's at stake]?`
Always a specific named thing. Never a rhetorical question.

**4. The negation.**
Model: *"Not Your Boring Ag Speaker, Damian is:"* (home)
Also: *"What you won't get:"* (boasg)
Formula: `Not [the category default]. [What you get instead].`
Use once per page maximum. It is positioning, not a personality.

**5. The collision.**
Model: *"A forward looking futurist meets an agricultural economist"* (FAQ, delivery style)
Formula: `[Role A] meets [Role B].`
Two credible identities in one line. The humor is in the pairing, not in a joke.

**6. The colon label.**
Model: *"Food Fear: How Fear is Ruining Your Dinner and Why You Should Celebrate Eating"* (book)
Also: *"Do Business Better: Traits, Habits, and Actions to Help You Succeed"* (book)
Formula: `[Short punchy name]: [the plain-English promise, spelled all the way out].`
For programs, books, and hub pages. The subtitle is allowed to be long, but it must be concrete.

**CTA microcopy.** He puts the benefit on the reader's side of the sentence and uses caps for emphasis rather than punctuation: *"Hire a Speaker that Works for YOU."*, *"Start Listening Today!"*, *"Add yourself to the list."*, *"Contact Damian today!"*, *"Book Damian"*. Verb first, second person, no more than five words.

## 8. The 10-item self-check

Run this against every paragraph before it ships. Any failure is a rewrite, not a note.

1. **Zero em dashes.** Grep the paragraph for `U+2014`, `&mdash;`, and `--`. If one exists, replace it with a period, a comma, or a colon. Colon preferred. Shell check: `grep -P '\x{2014}|&mdash;|--' <file>` must return nothing.
2. **Zero banned words.** Check against the Section 5 table, including "unlock", "elevate", "seamless", "robust", "leverage", "landscape", "delve", "passionate".
3. **Contraction density.** Count contractions and words. Under 1 per 100 words means the register is wrong. Target 1.5 to 2.
4. **A number is present, or none exists.** If the paragraph makes a claim about scale, reach, experience, or results, it must carry a figure from the source manifest. If it cannot, cut the claim.
5. **Sentence length varies.** At least one sentence of 8 words or fewer, and no more than one sentence over 35 words. Mean should land near 17.
6. **Length in range.** 2 to 4 sentences, under 70 words. If longer, split it.
7. **Second person appears.** At least one "you" or "your", or a named occupation ("growers", "meeting planners", "ag lenders"). "Professionals" and "stakeholders" do not count.
8. **No adjective stacks.** No tricolon of adjectives. Replace with one adjective, a number, or nothing.
9. **Specifics beat categories.** Every "companies", "audiences", "clients", "the industry" gets checked against the source for a real name, state, crop, or year that could replace it.
10. **The stage test.** Read it out loud as if from a stage in front of 400 farmers. If it sounds like a brochure, a LinkedIn post, or an AI, rewrite it. If a sentence could open a LinkedIn post, it is disqualified regardless of the other nine checks.

**Zero-tolerance items:** 1, 2, and 10. Everything else is a strong default with room for one deliberate exception per page.
